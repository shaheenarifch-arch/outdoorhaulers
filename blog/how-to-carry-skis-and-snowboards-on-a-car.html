Long, sharp-edged and often wet, skis and snowboards do not fit neatly into most cars once passengers, boots and bags are aboard. This guide explains how to carry skis on a car safely, compares the three main hauling setups (a roof box, a roof rack with ski carriers, and a hitch-mounted rack or cargo carrier) and covers the winter-specific checks that keep your gear, your vehicle and other drivers safe.

Quick answer

For most drivers, the best way to carry skis and snowboards on a car is a lockable roof box on crossbars: it keeps gear dry, out of road salt and out of sight. Clamp-style ski carriers on crossbars cost less and suit quick day trips, while a hitch-mounted carrier is easier to load but exposes gear to slush and spray.

Key takeaways

  • A roof box is the most protected option. Skis, poles and boards ride enclosed, locked and away from salty road spray, but you need crossbars and enough interior length.
  • Roof-mounted ski carriers are the simplest rack option. They clamp skis or boards to crossbars, load quickly and cost less than a box, but gear is exposed to weather.
  • Hitch carriers are the easiest to load. Nothing has to be lifted overhead, which helps on tall SUVs and icy lots, but the gear sits directly in the spray zone behind the car.
  • Winter adds its own checks. Clear snow off the load, re-check fasteners after the first few miles, rinse off road salt and keep lock cylinders from freezing.

What's the best way to carry skis and snowboards on a car?

It depends on what your car already has, how many people and boards you carry, and how much winter driving you do.

If you already have crossbars, or you are willing to add them, a roof box is usually the most versatile winter choice. It carries skis, snowboards, poles and soft bags in one locked space, keeps them clean, and frees up the cabin for passengers and boots. If you only ski occasionally and want something simpler, clamp-style ski carriers on the same crossbars get the job done for less. If lifting gear overhead is a problem, or your vehicle is tall, a hitch-mounted carrier is the most comfortable to load, provided you accept that your gear will collect road grime.

Option 1: a roof box for skis and snowboards

A roof box (also called a rooftop cargo carrier or ski box) is an aerodynamic hard shell that mounts to crossbars. For winter sports it has clear advantages:

  • Weather protection. Snow, sleet and the salty slush thrown up by traffic stay off your bases, edges and bindings.
  • Security. Most hard-shell boxes lock, so you can leave skis on the car during a lunch stop or overnight at a lodge with less worry.
  • Mixed loads. Skis, a board and poles share one space, keeping wet gear out of the cabin.

What to check before you buy a roof box

  • Interior length versus your longest ski or board. Volume in cubic feet does not tell you whether skis fit. Adult skis are commonly somewhere around 150 to 190 cm long and many snowboards fall roughly between 140 and 165 cm, so compare the box's listed internal length with your own gear rather than assuming.
  • Crossbar spread and mounting hardware. The box's clamps must match the spacing and profile of your crossbars.
  • Rear-hatch clearance. Slide the box far enough forward that a raised liftgate does not hit it.
  • Total height. A box adds height, which matters for garages, parking structures and drive-throughs.

Option 2: a roof rack with ski and snowboard carriers

The classic ski rack is a pair of padded clamps that bolt onto crossbars. Skis are pressed together base to base and held between rubber strips; snowboards usually lie flat, bindings facing down. Carriers come in different widths, sized by how many pairs of skis or boards they hold.

Why drivers choose ski carriers

  • Lower cost than a full roof box in most cases.
  • Quick loading once you get used to the clamp.

The trade-offs

  • Exposure. Gear rides in the open, so it collects snow, road film and salt on long drives.
  • Security. Many carriers lock, but the skis themselves are visible.

Start with the right crossbars

Whether you choose a box or clamp carriers, both mount to crossbars, so the bars are the foundation of the whole setup. Crossbars are usually specific to a vehicle and rail type. Option 3: a hitch rack or hitch cargo carrier

If your vehicle has a receiver hitch, you can carry winter gear behind the car instead of on top. There are two broad approaches:

  • Hitch-mounted ski and snowboard racks. These hold skis or boards upright or angled in clamps on a mast that slides into the receiver. They are a specialty item; check the manufacturer's capacity and whether the rack tilts or swings away so you can still open the liftgate.
  • Hitch cargo carriers. A basket or tray carrier can hold a ski bag, boot bags and duffels. Long skis may overhang the sides of a tray, so measure first and never let gear stick out beyond the width of the vehicle.

Advantages in winter

  • Loading at waist height is safer on icy ground than reaching over a tall roof.
  • No added height, so garages and parking structures are not a concern.
  • Better fuel economy: the U.S. Department of Energy notes rear-mounted carriers generally affect mileage less than roof-top boxes.

Disadvantages in winter

  • Road spray. The area behind a moving car is where slush, sand and salt brine collect. Gear on a hitch will get dirty unless it is bagged.
  • Exhaust heat. Keep bases, bags and plastic parts well clear of the tailpipe.
  • Visibility. The load can block taillights and the license plate; many states require both to stay visible.
  • Tongue weight. The carrier and everything on it count toward your hitch and vehicle tongue weight rating.

Can you carry skis inside the car?

Yes, and for one or two people it is often the simplest choice. Many sedans have a ski pass-through behind the rear armrest, and most SUVs and hatchbacks can fit skis diagonally with one rear seat folded. A few habits make it safer:

  • Put skis in a bag so edges do not cut upholstery and melting snow does not soak the carpet.
  • Secure them with cargo anchors or straps. Loose skis can become dangerous projectiles in a sudden stop.
  • Keep the driver's view through the rear window and mirrors clear.

How to choose: a quick decision checklist

How to use this checklist: answer each question in order. The first "yes" that rules an option out narrows your choice quickly.

  1. Do you have roof rails and crossbars? If not, a roof box or ski carriers means buying crossbars first. If you have a hitch, a hitch carrier may be quicker.
  2. How many people ride? Four people with gear usually need the cabin for passengers, which pushes skis outside.
  3. How long is your longest ski or board? Write it down in centimeters and inches and compare it to every listing's internal or usable length.
  4. How much salted highway do you drive? Lots of it favors an enclosed box or bagged gear.
  5. What is your roof load limit and tongue weight rating? Look them up in the owner's manual before you load anything.

How to load skis and snowboards step by step

  1. Clear snow and ice from the rack, box lid and roof before loading. Packed snow adds weight and can hide a loose clamp.
  2. Knock snow off boots, skis and boards so meltwater does not pool inside a box or the cabin.
  3. Load the heaviest items first and center them between the crossbars or over the hitch, then add lighter items.
  4. Follow your rack maker's orientation guidance. On clamp carriers, many manufacturers recommend skis with tips pointing toward the rear to reduce wind lift; snowboards typically go bindings down.
  5. Close and lock everything, then tug on the load from several directions. Nothing should shift.
  6. Check lights, plate and rear camera on hitch setups.
  7. Stop after the first few miles and re-check clamps, straps and the hitch pin. Cold temperatures can change how straps and rubber pads behave.

Winter driving and loading safety

Respect weight limits

Your owner's manual lists a roof load limit and a tongue weight rating, and racks, boxes and carriers have their own ratings. Always go by the lowest number in the chain. On the roof, the limit includes the crossbars and box themselves, not just the skis. On the hitch, the carrier plus its contents counts as tongue weight. If you are unsure, our road-trip hauling guide explains these terms in plain English.

Check fasteners before every trip

  • Crossbar towers and clamps: tight, with no cracks in plastic covers.
  • Box mounting clamps: fully engaged and locked.
  • Ski clamp rubber: not torn or brittle from cold.
  • Hitch pin and clip, or locking pin: in place and secure.

Drive for the load

  • Allow more stopping distance. Extra weight and slick roads both lengthen braking.
  • Watch crosswinds. Roof loads catch wind on open highways and bridges; slow down when gusts pick up.
  • Remember your height. A note on the dashboard can save a roof box from a low garage beam.
  • Clear the whole vehicle. Snow sliding off a roof box onto the windshield, or onto the car behind you, is dangerous. Some states have laws requiring drivers to clear snow and ice from their vehicles.

Salt and ice care for racks, boxes and gear

Road salt and de-icing brine are hard on metal and moving parts. A little maintenance goes a long way:

  • Rinse after salty drives. Use fresh water on crossbars, clamps, hitch carriers and the receiver, then let them dry. Pay attention to bolts and pivot points.
  • Dry skis and boards. Wipe edges and bases dry at home to limit rust on steel edges.
  • Protect locks from freezing. Keep lock cylinders covered when possible and use a lock lubricant made for cold weather. Avoid pouring hot water on a frozen lock; it can refreeze worse.
  • Open frozen lids gently. Let the car warm up, tap ice loose and do not force hinges or latches.
  • Inspect in spring. Look for corrosion, cracked plastic and stretched straps before storage.

Bottom line

If you want the most protected, all-weather way to carry skis and snowboards on a car, choose a lockable roof box on crossbars that is long enough for your gear. For quick, low-cost day trips, clamp-style ski carriers on the same bars work well. If lifting overhead is the problem, a hitch-mounted rack or cargo carrier with bagged gear is the easiest to load. Whatever you pick, stay within every weight rating, re-check fasteners after the first few miles and rinse off road salt when you get home.

Frequently asked questions

What's the best way to carry skis and snowboards on a car?

For most drivers, a lockable roof box on crossbars is the best all-round choice because it keeps gear dry, clean and secure. Clamp-style ski carriers cost less and suit day trips, and a hitch carrier is easiest to load but exposes gear to road spray.

Do I need crossbars to carry skis on my car roof?

Yes. Roof boxes and clamp-style ski carriers both mount to crossbars. If your car has roof rails but no bars, add crossbars that match your vehicle and rail type first, and stay within the lower of the bar rating and your vehicle's roof load limit.

Will my skis fit in a roof box?

Only if the box's internal length is longer than your longest ski or board. Cubic-foot volume does not tell you this, so measure your skis and compare them with the internal length on the box listing before you buy.

Is it safe to carry skis on a hitch rack in winter?

It can be, as long as the rack is rated for the load, the hitch is secure and lights and the license plate stay visible. Gear behind the car collects salty slush, so bag skis and boards and rinse everything after the drive.

How do I stop road salt from damaging my rack and skis?

Rinse racks, clamps, hitch parts and the receiver with fresh water after salty drives and let them dry. Wipe ski edges and bases dry at home, and use a cold-weather lock lubricant to keep lock cylinders working.

Does a roof box affect fuel economy?

Yes. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that roof-top cargo boxes add drag and can noticeably reduce fuel economy, especially at highway speeds, while rear-mounted carriers generally have a smaller effect. Remove the box when you no longer need it.
