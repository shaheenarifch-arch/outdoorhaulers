Quick answer

When you are towing a trailer or hauling bikes, kayaks or a cargo carrier, plan campground stops around three things: how far you can comfortably drive with the extra load, whether the site fits your rig, and where your gear will be safe overnight. Most haulers do best with shorter driving days (often 250 to 350 miles), pull-through sites for one-night stops and campgrounds with secure, well-lit sites. Highway-friendly campgrounds such as KOA Journey locations are built for exactly this kind of overnight stop, while KOA Holiday and Resort campgrounds suit longer stays at your destination.

Key takeaways

  • Hauling changes your pace. Expect slower speeds, more fuel stops and longer braking distances than normal driving.
  • Book sites that fit your total length. Measure vehicle plus trailer or rack plus anything hanging off the back.
  • Pull-through sites save stress. They let you arrive late and leave early without reversing a trailer.
  • Secure gear overnight. Lock racks, use cable locks on bikes and kayaks and keep valuables out of sight.
  • Use a checklist at every stop. Hitch pins, straps and lights loosen over long miles.

Plan realistic driving days

A loaded vehicle does not travel like an empty one. Trailers and hitch racks add weight and wind resistance, which means more fuel stops, slower climbs and longer braking. Fatigue builds faster too, because you are watching mirrors and leaving more space.

  • Cap your daily distance. Many towing drivers aim for roughly 250 to 350 miles a day. Fewer miles means arriving in daylight with energy to set up.
  • Arrive before dark. Backing in, leveling and hooking up are much easier in daylight.
  • Plan fuel stops ahead. Not every gas station has room for a trailer. Truck stops and larger stations are easier to enter and leave.
  • Build in a rest day. On trips longer than three or four days, a two-night stop lets you do laundry, check the rig and actually enjoy the trip.
  • Watch the weather. Crosswinds affect tall trailers, roof boxes and hitch racks. Slow down or wait out strong gusts.

Weight basics every hauler should know

Before you plan stops, make sure the load itself is within limits. These terms appear in your owner's manual and on hitch and rack labels:

Towing capacity
The maximum trailer weight your vehicle is rated to pull, usually listed in the owner's manual. It assumes the vehicle is otherwise properly loaded.
Tongue weight
The downward force the trailer or hitch accessory puts on the hitch ball or receiver. A hitch rack and the bikes on it count entirely as tongue weight. Many trailers ride best with tongue weight at around 10 to 15 percent of trailer weight; check your trailer and vehicle guidance.
GVWR
Gross vehicle weight rating: the maximum your vehicle can weigh fully loaded, including passengers, cargo and tongue weight.
Roof load limit
The weight your roof and crossbars can carry while driving, including the rack, box or cradles themselves.

A public scale at a truck stop is an inexpensive way to check your real loaded weight before a big trip.

Add up everything honestly: people, coolers, firewood, water in tanks, bikes and the rack itself. Many drivers are surprised how quickly a family road trip reaches the limit. If you are close, ship or leave behind heavy items, or split gear between the hitch and roof within both ratings.

Weather and terrain on the road

  • Mountain passes. Use lower gears on long descents to save your brakes, and expect slower climbs with a full load. Check pass closures in shoulder seasons.
  • Crosswinds. Trailers, roof boxes and kayaks catch wind. On exposed plains and bridges, slow down, keep both hands on the wheel and consider waiting out strong gust warnings.
  • Heat. Hot weather adds strain on engines, transmissions and tires. Check tire pressure when tires are cold and watch gauges on long climbs.
  • Rain and road spray. Cover cargo, leave extra following distance and check that lights are clean and visible.
  • Gravel campground roads. Drive slowly, watch for low branches and ask the office about the easiest route to your site.

Sample plan: a 5-day hauling road trip

Day 1: short start

Leave after a full check of the hitch, straps and lights. Drive 200 to 250 miles to a highway campground with a pull-through site. A short first day lets you spot any loose straps or handling issues early.

Day 2: the long leg

Drive up to 350 miles with fuel and stretch stops every couple of hours. Arrive by late afternoon, hook up electric only and keep the rig hitched if you leave early.

Days 3 to 5: destination stay

Settle into a destination campground for three nights. Unhitch, level properly and unload bikes and kayaks. Use the rest day for laundry, a rig check and the activities you hauled the gear for. On departure day, run the full departure checklist before leaving.

Shift the plan to match your vehicle, experience and the terrain. The key idea is simple: shorter days going out, a proper stay at the destination and no late-night arrivals.

Site details to confirm before booking

  • Maximum length. Include your tow vehicle and everything on the hitch.
  • Pull-through or back-in. Pull-through is best for overnight stops; back-in sites can be quieter for longer stays.
  • Hookups. Electric (and amp rating), water and sewer, depending on your rig.
  • Parking for the tow vehicle. Some sites fit a trailer but not a second vehicle alongside.
  • Surface and level. Gravel or paved pads level more easily than grass; bring leveling blocks either way.
  • Extra vehicle or gear rules. Ask whether bikes and kayaks can stay on the site or need to be stored elsewhere.

Hauling bikes, kayaks and cargo to camp

Bikes and e-bikes

A hitch rack keeps bikes off the roof and easy to load. E-bikes are heavy, so check your rack's per-bike weight limit and your hitch's tongue weight. Remove e-bike batteries for long highway runs if the manufacturer recommends it, and store batteries indoors overnight in cold weather. See our guide to e-bike hitch racks for heavy electric bikes.

Kayaks

Kayaks usually ride on the roof with J-cradles or saddles, or on a trailer for multiple boats. Use bow and stern lines on longer trips and recheck straps after the first 20 to 30 minutes. Our kayak roof rack guide explains the options.

Cargo carriers

Hitch cargo baskets and roof boxes free up cabin space for passengers. Pack heavy items low and forward, cover the load against rain and road spray and make sure lights and the license plate remain visible.

Combining loads

Many families carry bikes on the hitch and a kayak on the roof. That combination works if you stay within both the roof and hitch ratings. Our guide to a hitch mount rack for e-bikes and kayaks shows how to split the load.

Arrival checklist at the campground

  1. Check in and ask about your site. Confirm site number, the best approach route and any low branches or tight turns.
  2. Walk the site first. Look for hookups, slope, overhead branches and the best spot to park the tow vehicle.
  3. Position and level. Use a spotter and agreed hand signals when backing. Chock wheels before unhitching.
  4. Connect utilities in order. Surge protector and electric first, then water with a pressure regulator, then sewer if needed.
  5. Unload and lock gear. Take bikes and kayaks off or lock them to the rack with cable locks.
  6. Do a quick rig inspection. Check tire pressure, hitch pin, safety chains and lights while everything is still fresh in mind.

When a cabin beats a campsite

You do not have to tow a camper to use campgrounds on a hauling trip. If you are carrying bikes, kayaks or a cargo carrier but not a trailer, a camping cabin can be the best of both worlds. You park right next to the cabin, keep an eye on your gear, sleep in a real bed and skip setting up a tent after a long drive. Deluxe cabins add a bathroom and kitchen, which helps on multi-day stays.

Cabins also make sense when weather turns. If storms are forecast, switching one night from a tent site to a cabin keeps the trip on track. Check whether bedding is included and whether the cabin site has room for your vehicle with a hitch rack attached.

Departure checklist

  • Disconnect and stow hoses and power cords; close tank valves
  • Retract steps, awnings and stabilizers; secure loose items inside
  • Hitch up: coupler latched, pin and clip in, safety chains crossed, breakaway cable attached, brake controller working
  • Reload and strap bikes, kayaks and cargo; check every lock and strap
  • Walk around: lights, turn signals, brake lights and tire pressure
  • Final site sweep for chocks, blocks and small items

Keeping gear secure overnight

Bikes, kayaks and cargo carriers are easy targets because they sit outside. A few habits reduce the risk:

  • Lock to something solid. Use a cable or chain lock through bike frames and kayak grab handles to the rack, and lock the rack to the hitch.
  • Use hitch and rack locks. A locking hitch pin stops the whole rack from being removed.
  • Cover it up. A simple cover hides expensive bikes from view.
  • Choose well-lit, staffed campgrounds. Campgrounds with on-site staff and lighting feel safer than remote pull-offs.
  • Bring batteries and small valuables inside. E-bike batteries, GPS units and bags should not sit on the rack overnight.
  • Photograph serial numbers. If anything goes missing, you have the details for a report and insurance claim.

Saving money on a hauling road trip

  • Join a campground rewards program if you stop often. KOA Rewards members save 10% on stays and earn points; it has an annual fee, so it pays off fastest on multi-night trips.
  • Drive a little slower. Lower speeds with a trailer or roof load can noticeably improve fuel economy.
  • Remove racks and boxes when not needed. Empty racks still add drag.
  • Cook at camp. Grocery stops beat restaurant meals for the whole family.
  • Travel midweek or shoulder season. Campgrounds are quieter and easier to book.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Booking a site that is too short. Always include racks and anything that sticks out.
  • Driving too far in one day. Late arrivals and tired drivers lead to scraped trailers and stressful setups.
  • Skipping strap checks. Straps stretch and loads settle, especially in the first hour.
  • Leaving batteries on bikes overnight in the cold.
  • Forgetting your height. Roof loads can hit low branches, drive-thrus and parking garages.

Limitations and checks

Towing and roof-load limits come from your vehicle manual, hitch and rack manufacturers, so always follow those ratings. Campground site sizes, hookups and gear rules vary by location; confirm details with the campground before you book.

Frequently asked questions

How many miles a day should I drive when towing a trailer?

Many towing drivers aim for about 250 to 350 miles a day so they can arrive in daylight and set up without rushing. Adjust for terrain, weather and how experienced you are.

What is a pull-through campsite?

A pull-through site lets you drive in one end and out the other without reversing. It is ideal for trailers and one-night stops.

Can I leave bikes on my hitch rack overnight at a campground?

Often yes, but lock bikes to the rack, lock the rack to the hitch, cover them and bring e-bike batteries inside. Check the campground's rules too.

How do I know if my trailer fits a campsite?

Measure your total length including the tow vehicle, trailer and anything on the hitch, then compare it with the site's maximum length when you book.

Is KOA good for overnight stops when towing?

KOA Journey campgrounds are designed for convenient stops near highways, and many offer pull-through sites. Check each location's site details.

Do I need full hookups for one night?

Not always. For one night, electric is often enough if your fresh-water and holding tanks are ready. Longer stays benefit from full hookups.
